**Verdict (question 1): No, not sufficient on its own as evidence of research capability for a frontier lab.** It is strong evidence of unusual methodological discipline—pre-registration, selection-bias correction, negative controls, honest reporting of falsified endpoints—but it is weak evidence that the researcher can do the substantive work a lab cares about: detecting a model’s *own* hallucinations in free generation. The central construct is input-label discrimination, the thresholds are too weak to be deployment-relevant, and there are no comparisons to simple baselines. A reviewer would notice the rigor first, then ask why the paper spends so much care measuring something that may be a proxy.

## 1. Sufficiency: positive and negative first impressions

**Positive.** The execution is genuinely above average for an independent researcher. The nested out-of-bag selector that re-selects and sign-locks inside each resample is the right way to avoid selection bias. The shuffled-label negative control passing is meaningful. The row- and cluster-level bootstrap is correct for paired data. The pre-registered failures—18/20 against a stricter bar, A2 failing by sign inversion, both replication endpoints failing—are reported rather than hidden. That is rare and credible.

**Negative.** The paper’s headline is built on weak definitions of success. “Deployable” is defined as an OOB AUROC 95% CI lower bound above **0.50**. A detector with an AUROC of 0.51 would technically clear that. The “universal floor” is only an above-chance bar of 0.55, and held-out AUROCs are reported as low as 0.54. This is not a floor a deployment team can use.

The first thing a serious reviewer would ask is: **why are the labels always supplied in the prompt?** The second is: **why are there no simple baselines against which these 29 signals must prove added value?** A linear probe on final hidden states, a logistic regression on lexical features, or even input-level contradiction markers might perform comparably. Without that comparison, the representational content claim is incomplete.

The B1 replication failure also raises a judgment question. The pre-registered systematic-abort rule disqualifies an entire task because one model emits a few non-canonical commitments out of 1,000 rows. That is a defensible pre-registration decision, but a reviewer may read it as a brittle protocol rather than a scientific finding.

## 2. The construct limitation: does it sink the work?

It does not sink the work **if the work is scoped exactly as the abstract now states**; it bounds the work to a representation-measurement programme, not hallucination detection.

The limitation is real and severe. HaluEval, ANLI, and TriviaQA-paired all place the candidate under judgment in the prompt. The model is deciding whether a supplied answer is faithful/entailing/correct relative to supplied evidence. That is a reading-comprehension or judgment task, not a free-generation hallucination task.

For a frontier lab, this matters enormously. Hallucination detection is usually about cases where the model *itself* generates an unsupported answer. This paper does not test that. The geometry may be encoding properties of the input pair—lexical overlap, contradiction markers, surface recognizability—rather than the model’s internal uncertainty about its own outputs.

So the limitation should be stated as: **it disqualifies the paper as evidence of hallucination-detection capability, but it does not invalidate the methodological side if the paper is framed as input-label discrimination.** The author has already moved the framing in that direction, but many sections still use the language of “hallucination constructs” and “faithfulness,” which will keep the mismatch visible.

## 3. What raises it most per unit of effort?

Ranked by cost-to-credibility impact:

1. **Add a free-generation condition, even small.**  
   Use the same commit-moment extraction on model-generated answers to a QA/factuality task. Labels can come from a judge model or a small manual set. Even \(n=300\)–\(500\) rows would change the question from “does the model recognize a supplied label?” to “does the geometry predict the model’s own errors?” This is the single highest-impact addition. Without it, the paper cannot escape the proxy label.

2. **Add a simple baseline comparison.**  
   At minimum, train a logistic regression or linear probe on the final hidden state or a few hand-selected features of the input. If the 29-signal panel does not beat that probe under the same nested OOB procedure, the panel is not contributing much. This is cheap and directly addresses the most obvious reviewer criticism.

3. **Make the paper self-contained.**  
   The signal definitions currently depend on companion manuscripts “in preparation.” A reviewer cannot verify the work from the paper alone. Move full derivations or a succinct appendix into CC. This is text work, not compute.

4. **Replace weak thresholds with practical operating points.**  
   Report precision-recall curves, AUROC at fixed false-positive rates, or an actual decision threshold with confusion matrices. The current “CI lower bound > 0.50” and “above-chance 0.55 floor” framing is technically honest but practically unpersuasive. A deployment team wants to know what happens at 5% FPR, not whether the signal beats chance.

5. **Keep the audit record separate.**  
   The append-only audit log and withdrawal history should be a linked supplement, not part of the main narrative. It supports credibility without making the paper about past errors.

## 4. Is the audit record an asset or a liability?

**An asset, but only as a supplementary process artifact.**

It reads as rigor if the reader sees it as: *this person pre-registers, keeps an append-only log, catches errors, and withdraws claims rather than defending them.* That is exactly the behavior a lab wants in a researcher focused on evaluation or safety.

It reads as instability if the main document leads with a saga of withdrawn claims, reversed gates, and false statements. That makes the current results feel provisional. So the audit record should be available—repo, log, summary—but not foregrounded.

A one-line cover note such as “all prior findings are audited in the open; see repo/log” is enough.

## 5. What is missing that a serious reader would expect?

A serious reader would expect to find, and would not find:

- **No free-generation hallucination test.**  
  The paper measures input-label discrimination only.

- **No baseline comparison.**  
  No linear probe, no lexical baseline, no off-the-shelf confidence-only model comparison. The paper cannot yet claim the geometric panel adds value.

- **No practical effect sizes.**  
  AUROC lower bounds above 0.50 and an above-chance floor of 0.55 are weak. A lab will want precision/recall or false-positive-rate-controlled numbers.

- **No contamination analysis.**  
  ANLI, TriviaQA, and HaluEval are public and likely in some pretraining corpora. The paper should at least discuss contamination risk for the models used.

- **No frontier-scale evidence.**  
  The cohort is mostly 1.7–8B open-weight models, often 4-bit MLX. That is fine for a floor study, but a frontier lab will discount any universality claim made at that scale.

- **No direct comparison to existing methods like semantic entropy or HARP on the same data.**  
  The related-work section mentions them, but the empirical section does not test against them.

- **No self-contained signal definitions.**  
  The paper refers to “companion reports” for derivations, which prevents independent verification.

## 6. Would I send this?

**No, not as the primary evidence.** I would not send it as the centerpiece of a research-capability portfolio.

If it is sent now, it should be sent **only as a process artifact**, with a short cover note that says something like:

> This is a pre-registered measurement study of prompt-supplied input-label discrimination. It demonstrates my working method—pre-registration, nested OOB selection, negative controls, provenance, honest failure reporting—not a claim to have solved hallucination detection. Code, matrices, and audit log are at [repo].

That framing protects the author from the obvious criticism that the paper studies a proxy.

To make it a sufficient primary artifact, two things must exist first:

1. **A free-generation condition** testing whether the same geometry predicts model-own errors.
2. **At least one simple baseline comparison** showing the panel adds information over raw hidden states or lexical features.

With those additions, the paper would be a genuinely strong independent-lab portfolio piece: careful, honest, pre-registered, and relevant. Without them, it is a disciplined but bounded representation-measurement study, and a frontier lab will read it as such.
